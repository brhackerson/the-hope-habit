# Not a Turnover
## What a Shot Clock Taught Me About Hope

Recently, my doctor explained something that reframed the last several months for me. Certain results of my training have been stalling, and I learned that day that it isn't stubbornness. It's biology: Some old survival instinct still convinced the body it might need that reserve someday. I have also learned through my own research that my protein needs have shifted to protect the muscle I've built. There's a choice I made too: Doubling down on the same aggressive approach I used last year isn't something I want to impose on the people I share meals with, so I've deliberately eased off it this time around, on my own terms.

None of that matched what I expected. The first eight months of this were fast and predictable, same effort in, steady results out, almost every week. Eighteen months in, I'm putting in the same effort and getting a very different result. Some of it is genuinely better: strength and fitness I couldn't have imagined a year ago, and blood work that came back entirely in the normal range, a result I couldn't see in the mirror but the doctor could see on paper. Some of it falls short of what I was quietly expecting, based solely on how the first phase went.

## The realization: relative sizing broke my model

In Scrum, we don't estimate how long something will take. We estimate size, relative to something we already understand, and let the sprint cadence turn that into a timeline. Teams do this by picking a reference story, a piece of past work everyone already knows well enough to size new work against: Is this bigger or smaller, harder or easier, than that? Humans are bad at guessing absolute time. We're good at comparing something new to something we've already lived through.

Without meaning to, I'd done exactly that with my own body: phase one became the reference story for phase two. That's relative sizing. It's usually a good bet.

It broke here because the system itself had changed underneath me. A team's velocity doesn't stay constant forever, not when the makeup of the team shifts. Neither does a body's pace, not eighteen months into rebuilding itself. I kept comparing the current work against a reference point that no longer existed.

This isn't just a body thing. Most of us do it with hope itself. You recover from one disappointment in a matter of days, a rejection, a plan that falls through, and some part of you quietly files that away as the reference story: This is how long recovering takes. Then a bigger disappointment lands, and when a few days pass, and you're still not over it, the same sizing kicks in, except this time it tells you something is wrong with you, instead of telling you the two situations were never close to the same size.

Here's the part that actually stings: A broken time estimate doesn't announce itself as a broken estimate. It just feels like the practice stopped working, and the inner critic stands ready to make that case. The frustration and doubt it stirred up showed up right on schedule, and none of it was really about whether the practice was working. It was about a clock I'd built out of a comparison that had quietly stopped applying.

## Ditch the shot clock

A shot clock exists in the game of basketball to punish a team for taking too long, whether or not the possession deserved more time. It doesn't ask if the defense is good or the play is complicated. It just runs out, and you lose the ball.

Somewhere in the last several months, I started treating my own timeline like it was on a shot clock, and it was running down quickly. The inner critic was quick to call a missed pace a turnover, as evidence that the whole practice was failing, rather than what it actually was: an old estimate that no longer matched reality.

The hope practice was never playing under those rules. Nobody ends the game because your body needed more time than the first eight months did.

This isn't a new idea to me; it's one I already trust in other parts of my life. A sprint in Scrum has a boundary, but a story that doesn't finish inside it doesn't disappear or count against you. It rolls into the next sprint. Arnold Schwarzenegger's fitness newsletter landed in my inbox recently, making the same point about workouts: miss Monday's session in his Pump Club app, and it doesn't vanish; it just moves to Tuesday and waits for you. Both are the same rule wearing different clothes: cadence without punishment. That rhythm is what builds the consistency, not the fear of missing it. It's the return that does the work, not the threat of a buzzer.

## Before you call it broken

Once I understood the actual reasons the old comparison no longer applied, I could let the estimate go without losing sight of the goal. The number didn't need to defend itself against a timeline I'd invented. It just needed to keep moving, on whatever schedule the current version of my body actually runs on.

If some part of your life feels like it's losing to a clock right now, check the clock first. Ask what you're actually comparing it to, and whether that comparison still applies. Run the same check I did: what's changed in you since the last time this felt easy, what's changed around you, the people, the constraints, the stakes, and what you've chosen to do differently this time, on purpose, that the old timeline never accounted for. Any one of those is enough to invalidate the old comparison. You might find the practice was never broken. Just the estimate.

The hope practice doesn't have a shot clock. The only thing it requires is that you keep showing up for it, and for yourself.
