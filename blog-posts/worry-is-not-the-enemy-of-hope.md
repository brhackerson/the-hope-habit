# Worry Is Not the Enemy of Hope

*I kept showing up while the worry ran loud, and the practice held.*

I finished my last set, racked the bar, and picked up my phone. I flipped over to social media and let my pointer finger do the work, flicking the feed upward one post at a time. I scanned for one particular headline, the one I half hoped to see.

It never appeared, and the feed showed the same world it had shown yesterday.

That reading did not ruin my day. It stayed a reading, one data point from one morning, and I put the phone down and turned to what I could influence. On most days, that is what hope looks like in my life: steady and ordinary. It has not always come easily.

## The worry was real

I carried a worry that changed shape over the past 10 years. This fall an election attached itself to it. The stakes felt high to me, and I refused to pretend otherwise. This post does not argue for a party or a person, because the experience crosses every line. It describes what a long stretch of worry does to a hope practice, and what the practice does in return.

Some advice says to ignore the news. That approach does not fit me. When something matters deeply, looking away feels like abandoning it. Following the news and letting it run my day are two different acts, and my practice lives in the space between them.

## Worry is a signal of care

I worry only when something matters to me. This outcome mattered, and it sat outside my control.

So I stopped treating worry as an enemy to defeat. Fighting it creates one more contest, and my inner critic wins contests like that. I see the worry for what it is instead. The worry tells me what I value, and the story my critic attaches to it is a separate thing. "Same as yesterday" is a fact. "Nothing will ever change" is a story, and the critic slips it in so quietly that it feels like a conclusion.

My body kept its own score. On a recent Wednesday morning I woke up with a sense of ease. My breathing came easier, my body felt light instead of heavy, and getting up and over to the gym took no effort at all. I moved without the sense of incoming doom and gloom that had trailed me for months. I never told my body to worry, and I never told it to stand down. The shift told me the stress had been physical all along, and not only a story my critic told.

## Where worry takes over

Worry becomes a problem when it grabs the wheel, and for months mine had. The workouts still happened because consistency carried me, but they turned into box-checking. I completed each session without digging into the practice behind it, and my writing slowed to almost nothing. The silence here had a reason.

Worry never stopped me from showing up. It was absolutely a drag on the energy and depth I bring once I arrive. Box-checking counts as something. My consistency during that stretch protected the habit until the depth returned, and I no longer treat a thin season as proof that the practice failed.

## Where hope lives

I picture my practice as a ladder. Single readings sit on the bottom rungs: last night's sleep score, a body scan, a headline. Trends sit above them, then the outcomes I want, then my keyword, Pictures. The ladder leans against a wall, my North Star of strong and majestic, and every rung means something only because of that wall.

When I checked the news hoping for one specific headline, I pinned my hope to a single reading I could not influence. The nearest, freshest number pulls my attention down the ladder on its own. My keyword takes deliberate effort to lift it back up, which is why I say hope is something you do.

C.R. Snyder's Hope Theory describes two engines: agency, the belief that you can move forward, and pathways, the ability to find a route when something blocks the obvious one. Both require action. An election outcome gives anyone very little agency, so I placed my hope where my actions reach: my training, my sleep, my writing, and the people in front of me.

## The worry will find a new headline

The relief felt real, and I welcome it. It does not retire the practice. For months before that Wednesday, the practice carried me while no relief had come, and it carried me even though I did not know how things would turn out.

My worry has a long record of finding a new object, and it will find a new headline. When it does, the ladder and the wall will still stand, and I will climb the same way: one controllable at a time.

## For you

My worry had a headline. Yours has its own: a diagnosis, a job, a market, or a child. Your keyword differs from mine, and your practice looks different too. The question stays the same: where does your action reach, even on the days the worry runs loud?

I recognize that some of what makes my practice possible, the time, the resources, the support, not everyone has in equal measure. The tools look different for different people. The practice doesn't.

If you face something permanent, such as a terminal illness, a profound loss, or a condition that will not change, hope does not wait for a better reading, whether one comes or not. It meets the situation as it stands, and it finds the next small thing you can do.

## Why I wrote this

I started this post while the worry was still loud, because writing is a controllable, and I finished it after the worry eased. Putting these words down gave my hope somewhere to land, and it moved me one step back toward the practice I had been checking off a list.

Worry shows how much you care, and hope is what you do while you carry it.

Would you rather carry a result you cannot control, or a practice you can?
