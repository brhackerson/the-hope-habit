# What Falling Off a Paddleboard Taught Me About Hope
## You expand a comfort zone by walking straight into the edge of it, on purpose.

## The story

In the last month, I tried two things I'd have written off a year ago: hiking and standup paddleboarding.

Hiking wasn't hard, but it became something I actually enjoyed. A year ago, moving through rough terrain took everything I had, so the whole hike was about getting through it, not being in it. This time I could look around. The trail hadn't gotten easier. I had.

Standup paddleboarding was a different kind of new, and the idea wasn't even mine. Weeks earlier, my wife bought a paddleboard and went through the same learning curve herself. Watching her enjoy it was proof of how far her own health had come, since a year ago the sport likely wouldn't have been possible for her either. That's what got me on the board. A year ago I couldn't have imagined doing it. Yesterday I found out why it takes more than imagination: you don't get it right on the first try. It took several tries before I could even stand up on the board. I got on, I fell, I figured out what I'd done wrong, and I adjusted. Then I got back on. By the end of the session I was paddling instead of just recovering my balance every few seconds.

## The realization

That paddleboard session was the same loop I wrote about last month with food logging and Scrum, just standing on a lake instead of sitting in a sprint retro. Try something. See what happens. Inspect what went wrong. Adjust. Try again. I didn't expect to relearn transparency, inspection, and adaptation while soaking wet.

But here's the part that matters for hope specifically. I didn't get on that board already capable. My extended family was watching from the shore, and I was aware of it the whole time. A year ago, I wouldn't have subjected myself to that kind of embarrassment. This time I used their presence as motivation, proof that trying, failing, and adjusting in front of people is exactly how the process works. That's a comfort zone problem, not a competence problem, and you solve those two differently.

## Where hope actually lives

That idea isn't originally mine. Last winter, my daughter, who's working toward her PhD, tried something new as a total beginner and posted about what that felt like. It stuck with me on that lake: hope isn't just the confidence that you'll eventually get good at something. It's the permission to be bad at it in front of people, without the fear of failure or embarrassment deciding for you whether you try.

A comfort zone doesn't expand on its own. Mine didn't get bigger because I got older or generally more confident. It got bigger because I stood on a board I didn't know how to use, fell off it, and got back on anyway. Do that enough times, in enough different places, and the zone itself moves.

That's where I think hope actually comes from. Not from feeling ready. From evidence that you can walk into something you're not ready for and come out the other side able to do it. Hiking didn't require that from me anymore, because I'd already put in the work that made it comfortable. Paddleboarding still did, and that's exactly why it mattered more.

## An invitation for you

Pick one thing this week that sits just outside what you're comfortable with, not something you're already good at, something you'd have to fail at first to get right. Do it once. Then notice what happens to the size of the box you thought you lived in.

You don't grow a comfort zone by staying in it. You grow it by walking to the edge, on purpose, and taking one more step.
