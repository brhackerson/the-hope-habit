# What Empirical Process Control Taught Me About Hope
## Adapt what you can actually see, not what you assume.

## The story

If you've read my other posts, you've probably noticed how much I like data, especially now that it drives my hope practice. I use it to monitor progress, spot chances to improve, and know when to celebrate. After one of those celebrations, I stopped logging my food. I told myself I didn't need it anymore because I now had crossed into maintenance mode. I didn't stop tracking everything; I kept weighing in regularly, and my hope practice kept moving forward. My weight held steady while my muscle mass kept climbing. By any reasonable read, my practice was working.

Then I decided it was time to make some changes. Body weight maintenance was getting harder, even though everything else, strength, cardio fitness, was still moving in a positive direction. So I set a new goal: cut another 10 to 15 pounds to get to what I'd consider my ideal weight. Weeks went by, and nothing moved. The scale told me I was stable. It didn't tell me why I wasn't losing, or what to change. I formed a new hypothesis: you get what you measure, and measuring my food intake would drive improved results as it promoted mindfulness and intention in what I was putting into my body.

## The realization

Here's what I missed the first time: not every kind of tracking does the same job. A periodic check of my weight told me that I was mostly stable. However, it couldn't tell me what lever to pull for an updated, more specific target. I could see the outcome. I couldn't see the cause. The assumption underneath all of it was simple and wrong: if the outputs looked fine, the inputs must be fine too. Weighing in confirmed the outcome. It never tested the assumption.

Maintenance and a deliberate cut are two different problems, and they need two different kinds of information. General monitoring covers the first. It doesn't cover the second. Without food logging, I was flying blind on the one variable that actually mattered for the new goal.

The same is probably true for you. Whatever you're tracking in your own life, ask whether you built it for maintenance or for a harder push. Those aren't the same job, and assuming one covers the other is the mistake I made.

## Empirical process control

Looking through my professional lens, this is not a new concept for me. Scrum runs on what's called empirical process control: transparency, inspection, and adaptation.[¹] You can't inspect what isn't visible, and you can't adapt what you haven't inspected. Skip the first pillar and the other two collapse.

I'd applied that logic to sprints and backlogs for years before I applied it to my own body. Stopping the food log didn't just mean less data. It removed the transparency the whole loop depends on. Everything downstream of that quietly stopped working too, even while the parts I was still tracking looked fine.

You don't need to know Scrum to use this. Just ask, for whatever you're actually trying to change, can you see it closely enough to adjust it, or are you flying on a general impression?

## Real adversity: Mom's hospice care

This concept holds even when there's no cure to adapt toward. When my mother was in hospice, "how is she doing" wasn't enough for anyone to act on. What actually helped was specific, honest tracking: her pain levels, when she got medication, what worked and what didn't. My family struggled while working with hospice staff to get her pain under control. That struggle was really about transparency, watching closely enough to see what was actually happening instead of settling for a general impression.

That tracking never aimed at recovery. It gave us enough real information to make the next decision with care. The mechanism is the same one behind wheels spinning in my journey toward better overall health. The stakes are not.

## Not the same as having receipts

I've written before that receipts beat plans, that evidence matters more than a strategy you haven't tested yet. This is a step past that. Having receipts assumes you're still collecting them. The real risk isn't showing up without evidence. It's letting the collection quietly stop once things feel stable enough that you think you don't need it anymore. Stability can hide a stall for a long time before a harder goal exposes it.

## My takeaway

I get what I measure, whether I mean to or not. Stop measuring, and the practice doesn't stop; it just goes blind, or perhaps partially blind. The scale will tell me things are fine right up until the moment I need to know why they aren't.

That's the part of the hope practice most people skip. Hope isn't just deciding to feel hopeful. It's building enough transparency to keep adapting instead of assuming. Hope you build on assumption is optimism wearing a different name. Hope you build on what you can actually see is a practice you can trust.

## An invitation for you

If something in your life has felt stable for a while, that's worth a second look. Ask yourself what you stopped watching closely to get that read. Then watch it again, just for a week, and see what shows up.

There's a kindness in this too, not just a discipline. Cy Wakeman has a question worth borrowing: what do I know for sure?[²] Not what am I afraid of. Not what I am assuming. Just what do I actually know? That question only has a real answer when you have real information in front of you. Without it, you just guess, and guessing about yourself is where the inner critic lies in wait with thoughts of self-doubt. Good data closes that gap. It's a more decisive, less wishy-washy way to be gentle with yourself.

Adapt what you can actually see. Not what you assume.
