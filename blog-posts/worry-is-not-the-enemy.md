# Worry Is Not The Enemy

## Hope is what you do while you carry it

*Published: hopeisnotafeeling.substack.com, Nov 8, 2026*

---

I finished my last set, racked the bar, and picked up my phone. I flipped over to social media and let my pointer finger do the work, flicking the feed upward one post at a time. I scanned for one particular headline, the one I half hoped to see.

It never appeared, and the feed showed the same world it had the day before.

That reading did not ruin my day. It stayed a reading, one data point from one morning, and I put the phone down and turned to what I could influence. On most days, that is what hope looks like in my life: steady and ordinary. It has not always come easily.

## The worry was real

I carried a worry that changed shape over the past 10 years. This fall, an election attached itself to it. The stakes felt high to me, and I refused to pretend otherwise. This post does not argue for a party or a person, because the experience crosses every line. It describes what a long stretch of worry does to a hope practice, and what the practice does in return.

Some advice says to ignore the news. That approach does not fit me. When something matters deeply, looking away feels like abandoning it. Following the news and letting it run my day are two different acts, and my practice lives in the space between them.

## Worry is a signal of care

I worry only when something matters to me. This outcome mattered, and it sat outside my control.

So I stopped treating worry as an enemy to defeat. Fighting it creates one more contest, and my inner critic wins contests like that. Instead, I see worry for what it is. The worry tells me what I value, and the story my critic attaches to it is separate. “Same as yesterday” is a fact. “Nothing will ever change” is a story, and the critic slips it in so quietly that it feels like a conclusion.

My body kept its own score. On a recent Wednesday morning, I woke up with a sense of ease that surprised and delighted me. My breathing came easier, my body felt light instead of heavy, and getting up and over to the gym took no effort at all. I moved without the sense of impending doom and gloom that had trailed me for months, but seemed like years. I never told my body to worry, and I never told it to stand down. The shift told me the stress had been physical all along, and not only a story my critic told.

## Where worry takes over

Worry becomes a problem when it grabs the wheel, and for months mine had. The workouts still happened because consistency carried me, but they turned into box-checking. I completed each session without digging into the practice behind it, and my writing slowed to almost nothing. The silence here had a reason.

Worry never stopped me from showing up. However, it was absolutely a drag on the energy and enthusiasm I bring once I arrive. Box-checking counts as something, and during this time that was the win. I focused on stacking the daily boring wins, and not giving back all that I had built in my practice.

## Where hope lives

I picture my practice as an extension ladder. A ladder like that goes nowhere until you lean it against something, and the wall you pick decides where it takes you. My wall is my North Star, which is “strong and majestic.” Plenty of walls look alike, and my critic likes to swap in a look-alike when I’m not watching.

The rungs run from short term to long term, narrowing as they climb. The bottom rung is the widest because it holds the most: last night’s sleep score, a body scan, a headline, and a dozen more readings I can check before breakfast. One rung up, the trends narrow to the pattern across weeks of training and sleep. Above the trends sit the outcomes I want, and at the very top sits one word, Pictures, which reminds me why those outcomes matter.

The headline I checked that morning sat on the bottom rung. The nearest, freshest reading pulls my attention down the ladder on its own, so I end up staring at one rung and forgetting the rest. Climbing back takes deliberate effort, which is why I say hope is something you do: I move up one rung at a time. The headline gives me a single reading I cannot influence. The next rung up, I can access the weeks of training I have done, and the sleep I have protected. Higher up, my keyword of “Pictures” reminds me of the milestones I want to show up for. At the top, the ladder rests on the wall, and I ask the question that matters most: how can I show up strong and majestic today? That question has an answer I can act on, and the next headline cannot change it.

C.R. Snyder’s Hope Theory describes two engines: agency, the belief that you can move forward, and pathways, the ability to find a route when something blocks the obvious one. Both require action. An election outcome gives anyone very little agency, so I placed my hope where my actions reach: my training, my sleep, my writing, and the people in front of me.

## The worry will find a new headline

The relief felt real, and I welcome it. It does not retire the practice. For months before that Wednesday, the practice carried me even when no relief came, and it carried me even though I did not know how things would turn out.

My worry has a long record of finding a new object, and it will find a new headline. When it does, the ladder and the wall will still stand, and I will climb the same way: one controllable at a time.

## For you

My worry had a headline. Yours has its own: a diagnosis, a job, a market, or a child. Your keyword differs from mine, and your practice looks different too. The question stays the same: where does your action reach, even on the days the worry runs loud?

I recognize that some of what makes my practice possible- the time, the resources, the support is not available to everyone in equal measure. The tools look different for different people. The practice doesn’t.

If you face something permanent, such as a terminal illness, a profound loss, or a condition that will not change, hope does not wait for a better reading, whether one comes or not. It meets the situation as it stands, and it finds the next small thing you can do.

## Why I wrote this

I started this post while the worry was still loud, because writing is a controllable, and I finished it after the worry eased. Putting these words down gave my hope somewhere to land.

Worry shows how much you care, and hope is what you do while you carry it.

Would you rather carry a result you cannot control, or a practice you can?
