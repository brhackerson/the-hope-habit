# Hope Doesn't Need a Plan
## It needs a door you're willing to walk through.

## Show up once

In February 2025, I made a decision alone on a long drive home into a beautiful Minnesota winter sunset on the western horizon. I'd tried to address my health and fitness before, more than once. One attempt in 2020 was just getting off the ground when the pandemic hit, and my focus shifted to keeping myself and my family safe instead. The other attempts came with bigger plans: a ninety-day program, macros written out, a vision statement. None of it had stuck. The realization on that drive wasn't try harder. It was try smaller. I decided not to build a plan around it this time. I decided to do exactly one thing, and I meant it. Get up tomorrow morning and go to the gym to train.

That first morning was just logistics: which machines to use, how much weight to start with, how to set each seat, what to remember so the next session would go more smoothly. None of it had anything to do with inspiration. I stumbled through it, walked out, and didn't make it bigger than it was. No declaration. No reinvention story. Something had started, and that was enough.

The plan for day two was the same as day one: show up again. Then day three. I never sat down and designed the system I'm still running. I just kept figuring out how to show up one more time than the day before.

## A principle I already knew

Years ago, my collaborator Michaele Gardner and I built a framework called Agile Best Self, twelve principles adapted from the Agile Manifesto but aimed inward instead of at a team. The tenth one is easy to skim past: Simplicity, focusing on what energizes your best self, is essential. In the original Agile world, that principle is about maximizing the work you don't do. In ours, it's not just about doing less. It's about doing less of what doesn't matter so there's room left for what does.

I've coached people through that principle for years. Whether it's a team spending three months designing the perfect system before writing a line of code, or a person spending three months designing the perfect self-improvement plan before doing anything, the pattern is the same: the bigger the plan, the more of it there is to break before you ever find out if the idea was right.

I didn't run that principle on myself until I started building my hope practice. The advice everyone gives is to start before you feel ready, and that's true, but it undersells the actual problem. Most people who do start still build something too big to survive its first bad week: five workouts, a meal plan, a wake-up time, a journal, all launched on day one. Complexity is what breaks. Not motivation. Not readiness. The size of the thing you're asking yourself to maintain.

One workout was barely a decision. That's exactly why it survived the second day and the tenth.

## Stop waiting, start building the environment

Most people wanting more hope in their lives aren't actually stuck on timing. They're stuck on scale. They build a version of the plan complicated enough to look serious, and then an ordinary disruption, a bad night of sleep, a busy week, a bad mood, breaks one piece of it and takes the whole thing down. Hope isn't a precondition, and it isn't a big plan either. It's what shows up once you've built an environment simple enough to survive an ordinary day. James Clear makes a similar case in *Atomic Habits*: Environment shapes behavior more reliably than motivation does. Design the space, and the behavior gets easier to repeat than to skip.

You don't need the whole plan to do that. You need the smallest true version of it: one change, one decision, one door you choose to walk through on purpose, without knowing yet what day two looks like. Maximize the work you don't do up front. Let showing up again be the only plan you need.

## Before you build the whole plan

Find one thing in your life right now that you could improve, not overhaul, just improve, in a way that might let you see or feel something differently. Maybe it's the friendship you keep meaning to rebuild with one big gesture instead of a single text message, or the finances you keep meaning to fix with a whole new system instead of just opening the account and looking. If you're in the middle of real adversity right now, a diagnosis, a hard season with someone you love, this might look even smaller: not a fix, just one moment of real joy you go looking for tomorrow instead of waiting for it to find you. Don't design the ninety-day version. Do the smallest real version of it today.

That's hope showing up. And you made it happen. This is the hope practice, your hope practice.
