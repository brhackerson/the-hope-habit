# I Had the Will. I Didn't Have a Plan B.
## The hope practice needed a second route, not more willpower.

*Draft for hopeisnotafeeling.substack.com*

---

Every serious attempt at getting healthy before this one ran on an assumption I never said out loud: if the plan was right, the path would stay clear. A plateau, an injury, a bad week of sleep, a nutrition approach that stopped working, any of it meant the plan was wrong, or I was. The inner critic agreed instantly. It always does. And once the obstacle became a verdict instead of an obstacle, the whole effort came apart.

This time started differently. Trouble wasn't a possibility I hoped to avoid. It was a certainty I built the plan around. Not if something would go wrong, but what I'd do the moment it did.

That distinction sounds small. It isn't. C.R. Snyder's hope research splits hope into two engines: agency, the will to get somewhere, and pathways, the ability to find another way there when the obvious route closes.[¹] Every failed attempt before this one had agency. I wanted it enough. What I didn't have was pathways. So the first closed door ended the whole attempt, because I had no plan for what came after "no."

A few months in, the closed door showed up again, a genuine plateau this time. Weeks of consistent training and eating, and the numbers weren't moving. The old response would have been to read that stall as the practice failing. This time, the plan was never "this will work without a hitch." The plan was "something will block this, and then I'll find the next route."

So I asked for help: coaches, some research, and honestly, AI more than once, on mornings when no one else was awake to ask. None of it handed me a finished answer. What it gave me was enough of a shift to take the next step instead of stopping. A different rep range. A different way to think about the week's nutrition. A reason to keep going that didn't require the original plan to survive intact.

That's the part the Agile Manifesto got right long before I applied it to a body: responding to change over following a plan.[²] I used to think that value was about staying calm when bad news arrives. It's bigger than that. It's about being willing to change the plan itself, not just your read of it, the moment the plan stops working.

Reacting, in this version, looks like standing at the closed door and calling it the end. Responding looks like turning around and finding a door that's actually open, even if it wasn't the one on the original map.

None of this requires every obstacle to have a workaround waiting. Some walls really are walls. But most of what looked like a wall in my past attempts was just the first route closing, not the last one. I didn't need a better plan going in. I needed to expect the plan to break somewhere and already know what I'd do next when it did.

Trouble was never the question. It was going to show up either way. The only real question, the one that decides whether the attempt holds or collapses, is what you do the moment it arrives.
