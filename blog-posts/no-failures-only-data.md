# There Are No Failures, Only Data
## The hope practice is about responding over reacting.

*Draft for hopeisnotafeeling.substack.com*

---

I was standing at the InBody scanner in my gym, watching the results come up on the screen the way they do every few weeks. Two numbers caught my eye. My weight was down, which should have felt like good news. My muscle mass was down too, at the same time, which meant it wasn't. Losing weight while also losing muscle isn't progress. It's a smaller version of the same problem.

A trainer I consulted with was walking me through the results. I was still new to this kind of data and didn't yet know how to read it on my own. They're the one who caught the muscle mass number, suggested creatine, and explained why it could help given my age: past a certain point, the body starts losing lean muscle mass more quickly, and creatine supplementation can help slow that down.[¹] I'd done some research of my own before trying creatine, and it pointed me in the same direction as the trainer, along with a consistent recommendation to check with a doctor first. I took it to mine, who confirmed it was right for me. I added it. At first my weight went up, which was expected while my body adjusted to it. It wasn't long before both weight and muscle mass were moving in the right direction. What looked like a failure wasn't one. It was information, arriving early and looking worse than it actually was. A few scans later, those same two numbers had turned into a receipt: proof the correction had worked.

That's the idea behind this whole post. There are no failures in the hope practice. There's only data, telling you what to do next. In the Hope Cycle, the four-step rhythm behind this whole practice, that's the Recognize step: naming what actually arrived before deciding what it means. I believe that now. Learning how to actually act on it took longer.

The inner critic is the voice that runs a constant commentary on your limitations. It rarely waits for evidence before it delivers a verdict. That's not a malfunction. It's doing exactly what it's designed to do: move you back toward comfort and safety, away from anything that feels like risk. So it grabs the first number that looks bad and reacts immediately, before the rest of the data has a chance to show up. React first, ask questions never. That's the whole operating model.

Reacting is fast. It feels like decisiveness. It's usually wrong, because it runs on one data point instead of the trend, one moment instead of the whole picture.

Responding is different. Responding takes a beat. It asks what the data actually says, not just what it looks like it's saying in the first half second. That gap between reacting and responding is where the hope practice actually happens, and where the inner advocate, the voice that asks what's useful instead of what's wrong, does the work the critic refuses to do. The critic and the advocate can read the exact same number and reach opposite verdicts, the whole subject of an earlier post, *Before You Think That Hope Is Drifting*.

This isn't unfamiliar territory for me professionally. The Agile Manifesto says the same thing about entire projects: responding to change over following a plan.[³] The hope practice just applies it to a body scan instead of a sprint. Either way, the plan isn't sacred. The new information is.

I learned part of this from Dr. Michael Gervais's work on mindfulness: the ability to step outside yourself when the inner critic gets loud, and look at what's happening with curiosity instead of from inside it. Not agreeing with the critic. Not arguing with it either. Just creating enough distance to see it as a voice, not as the truth.

From that distance, I ask myself one question: is this reaction useful?

Most of the time, no. The reaction is just noise, the critic doing what it always does with an ambiguous number. Naming that, out loud if I have to, usually drains most of its power on the spot.

The second question came from Cy Wakeman, who studies drama in the workplace: what does great look like?[²] It's a strange question to ask in the middle of a bad-looking data point, and that's exactly why it works. It doesn't argue with the critic. It walks past it, toward something useful. It's a form of empathy toward yourself: redirecting instead of relitigating.

None of this makes the data painless. A number that looks bad still stings for a second. What changes is what happens in the second after that. React, and the critic writes the story before the evidence is in. Respond, and the data just becomes what it always was: information, telling you what to do next, and eventually, a receipt.

That's true whether the number shows up on a scale, in a work review, or in a doctor's office. The feedback will look bad sometimes before it looks true. The hope practice isn't about avoiding that feedback. It's about meeting it with a question instead of a verdict, the same practice behind *Comparison Didn't Steal My Joy. It Built It.*, just aimed at a single number instead of a whole trajectory.

Sometimes the data doesn't reverse no matter how you respond, a terminal diagnosis instead of a dip in muscle mass. When that's true, the question shifts. It stops being what does great look like to fix this and becomes what does great look like in how I show up for it, the ground *The Hope Practice Doesn't Require a Favorable Outcome* covers in full.

Back at the InBody scanner, the two numbers never changed what they said. What changed was what I did with them. That's easy to say about muscle mass. It's harder, and more necessary, to say about the numbers that don't move no matter what you do. Either way, the practice asks the same thing: read the data honestly, then decide what you're going to do about it, or how you're going to carry it. There are no failures. There's only data, and how you decide to meet it.
